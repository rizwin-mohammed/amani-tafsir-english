### Section – 23

*[Verses 183–185]*

*[p. 459]* The making of fasting, which is one of the five pillars of Islam, into law, and quite some matters concerning it, are the content of these verses. Let us here make a general survey of the matters there are to understand from them. The matters stated below are ones that can be understood in the light of these verses:

*[p. 460]* (1) It is calling, ‘O you who have believed’, that Allah says that fasting has been prescribed on you. Therefore, for every true believer fasting is obligatory. Only those who have been specially given exemption have exemption from this. In this matter there is no difference of opinion among the Muslims.

(2) The fasting in Islam is an abstinence consisting of giving up food and drink, and intercourse with women. It need not be said that this is generally something a little troublesome for everyone. In that respect, there is reason for this to be regarded as a burdensome law. That this is not a law fixed only for you, who are the community of Muhammad Nabi ﷺ, and that fasting had been prescribed in the earlier communities too, and that fasting need be observed only on a few days that have been determined – that these and so on have been stated is for removing this distress of mind.

(3) Because it has been said, ‘just as it was prescribed in the earlier communities’, it is not necessary that the fasting of the earlier communities and the fasting of this community should agree in all details. There is no objection to there being some little difference between the two.

(4) What Allah said as the aim to be achieved by fasting is **لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُون** (so that you may become those who observe caution). What is meant by observing caution is being people who have God-fearing reverence by way of observing Allah's injunctions and prohibitions. This matter is one that we have already stated earlier more than once. The sins and wrongs and offences that arise from man arise from the desires of his body, and from his passions, surely. Fasting, for its part, is moreover something that controls both of those. That is why the Prophet ﷺ advised young men who had no ability to marry to observe fasting, and said that fasting is a shield that wards off sins. (Bu; Mu.) The meaning of what has been said is not that by the time one observes one or two fasts it will hold the bodily passions under control. In case one observes fasting regularly, it is certain indeed that body and soul and mind will all become self-controlled. Fasting is not merely a bodily training, or only a purification in terms of health. There are also mighty gains, mental and spiritual, to be attained by means of it. But, fasting being nominal is not enough; it must be one that is indeed in the true sense. That is what the Prophet ﷺ pointed out in a hadith: ‘Whoever did not give up saying and doing wrongdoing, Allah has no need at all of his giving up his food and drink.’ (Bu.)

(5) That what is meant by having said ‘some numbered days’ (**أَيَّامًامَعْدُودَاتٍ**) is the days in the month of Ramadan is clear from the third verse. There is no long period at all. Only a few days are needed – pointing this out, what that word does is to show the burden of fasting as less.

(6) Allah *[p. 461]* has exempted the sick, and travellers, from the obligation of fasting. But, of what kind the sickness and the journey must be has not been explained. It has also been commanded that, as many days' fasting as was missed on account of them, so many in number must be fasted on other days to pay it off. That it must be a sickness such that one is unable to keep the fast, or such that, in case one observes fasting, some damage may occur to the body or to the limbs, and that it must be a journey of such distance that one is permitted to pray shortening the prayer (making it qasr) – is what many scholars are seen to have said. As for determining the distance for making the prayer qasr, there are moreover many differences of opinion. In the situation that there are no proofs such as to decide that among them only such and such is the correct one, and in the situation that Allah has not indicated any condition, it is enough if there is a sickness and a journey such as would be called, according to the ordinary usual way, ‘sick’ and ‘traveller’ in the true sense – is indeed another opinion. This is what seems more reasonable. (Allah knows)

But, one matter needs to be specially kept in mind. It is not to be interpreted even as far as this: that one who goes to buy goods in the market, and one who has caught a slight cold, and so on, may be reckoned as a traveller and as sick, and that they all may be exempted from fasting. One principle in having given relaxation to the sick and the traveller is the hardship that naturally arises for them in observing fasting, surely. Hardship, for its part, is also not a thing that can be fixed, measured and reckoned. Not only that, Allah has also not stated a condition, ‘if there is hardship’. In hadith too, it is not seen made clear. Therefore, it can be understood on the whole that, whether there is difficulty for fasting or not, there is no objection to the sick and the traveller dispensing with fasting, and that, in case there is difficulty, fasting must indeed be given up. When examining the hadiths, that too is what is understood. Some hadiths are also quoted below. **إن شاء الله**

(7) **وَعلى الذين يطيقونه فدية** ... *[DOUBT: the dots are printed at the left end of the Arabic, touching it; whether they belong before or after it cannot be told.]* (upon those for whom fasting is possible, there is the duty of a ransom) – about the import of having said this, there are two opinions. The opinion that most Quran commentators and scholars have accepted is like this: In the first stage, when fasting was made obligatory, it was experienced by the true believers as quite troublesome. (It was a little before the law of war came down, in the second year of the Hijrah, that fasting became law) Therefore, that was a relaxation that Allah permitted to them then. That is, for those who had the ability to keep the fast – for those who were not sick and travellers – it was enough if they observed fasting, or gave, in place of fasting, for each day the food of one needy person as a ransom. At the same time, it had also been made known that observing fasting is more excellent. Those who, not keeping the fast, made do with the ransom also need not afterwards fast that fast to pay it off. This is the import of that sentence. Later – it is said that it was after one year – in the next (185th) verse, (**فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ**) (in that month *[p. 462]* whoever was present, he, let him observe fasting in it) – with this having come down, this relaxation became annulled, and it became law that fasting must indeed be observed. Just as with many other laws of Islam, the law of fasting too was established gradually. The hadith that is the basis for this opinion is this:-

That Salamah ibn al-Akwa' (**سلمة بن الاكوع رضى الله عنه**) stated, it has come thus in all the chief books of hadith: **وَعَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُۥ فِدۡيَةٞ** (upon those for whom it is possible there is a ransom) – when this verse came down, those among us who intended to observe fasting would observe it, and those who intended to leave off fasting would leave it off and give the ransom. Until the verse after this, **فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ** (let those who are present in that month observe fasting), came down and did ‘naskh’ of it (annulled it), we went on doing so.’ (Bu; Mu; Da; Ti; Na; Ta. and others) The second opinion is that of Ibn 'Abbas (r) and others. That what was said, that those who have ability for fasting must give a ransom, is concerning women and men who meet with trouble in observing fasting because of old age – that Ibn 'Abbas (r) stated this, Bukhari (r) has recorded. That pregnant women who see that harm will befall the child in the womb because of fasting, and mothers who see that harm will befall the child that sucks the breast, are included in this group, has been narrated from some others too.

As Imam Raghib (r) stated, the meaning of **طَاقَة** (Taqat) is that it is what a man is able to do bearing a little hardship. The verb from this root is **يُطِيقُ** (Yutiqu). Along with that, there is also a reading here, **يُطَوَّقُونَ** (Yutawwaquna), and others. The meaning of this is to come to have ability with straining. It is taking all this into account that we have given the meaning ‘those who come to have ability (with straining) for it’ for the word **الذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ**.

After quoting the opinions seen above and their proofs, the relevant part of a statement that Imam Ibn Jarir (r) made can be quoted here:- **وَعَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُۥ -الخ** (upon those for whom it is possible for it, there is a ransom, namely giving the food of one needy person) – the best opinion in the explanation of this is that it is ‘mansukh’. Because, while it comes to be that giving a ransom is obligatory upon those who come to have ability for fasting, and at the same time all the Muslims have agreed that, among those who dwell in the homeland as healthy men, not being travellers, observing fasting is obligatory for all who have ability for fasting, and that giving a ransom without observing fasting is not permissible, it is certain that that verse is mansukh. Not only that, proofs that strengthen this, from Mu'adh, Ibn 'Umar, Salamah ibn al-Akwa' (r), *[p. 463]* we have also quoted before this, surely.’

On the whole it can be said thus: Old men and old women who have trouble in keeping the fast need not observe fasting. In case they have ability, they must give for each day the food of one needy person as a ransom. The problem of fasting afterwards to pay it off also does not affect them. Women who fear that harm will come about because of fasting, concerning children that are in the womb or that suck the breast, or concerning their own bodies, also need not keep the fast. But, there are opinions that it is enough if they give the ransom, and, on the contrary, that paying off by fasting when it becomes possible is needed as well, and that the ransom is not needed – what is needed is paying off by fasting. (**كمافى ابن كثير**) *[DOUBT: Arabic only, with no Malayalam rendering; it appears to mean “as in Ibn Kathir”.]* We are not venturing here into the explanations, and into the justifications of each one. Concerning the sick, and the travellers, Allah Himself has made it clear. There is also no dispute that fasting and the like are not obligatory for children who have not come of age and for those who have no power of discernment. Among the Muslims there has been no difference of opinion that, for all people other than those mentioned, observing the Ramadan fast is obligatory. Only in this recent time, some people, taking no account of any of the facts said above, taking into account only the outward meaning of this sentence, are wont to argue that the ruling in Islam is that it is enough if, in the month of Ramadan, one observes fasting, or gives a ransom instead. An air of boasting, that none of those before, other than themselves, was able to understand the Quran as it should be, or to evaluate the doctrines of the Quran, is also displayed in the words of these people. It has to be guessed that modern intoxication, a materialist mode of thinking, the interest in attracting public attention by way of new opinions, and the like, are what are behind opinions like these. In this age, when religious awareness is perishing even among religious believers, there will not fail to be people in this age to welcome any opinion by which laxity like this is obtained in religious injunctions, surely.

(8) That what must be given as the ransom is the food of one needy person (**طَعَامُ مِسْكِين**) is what Allah said. It has not been said that it must be food of such and such a kind, or that such and such a measure is needed. In that situation, what is justified is to hold that the foodstuff that is regarded as food in the ordinary course, and the measure that one person ordinarily needs to eat, will be enough. What has been said is in the place of one day's fast. For more days, the food needed for more needy persons must also be given. That Anas ibn Malik (r), when keeping the fast became troublesome for him because of advanced age, used to give for one or two years, every day, to one needy person each, meat and bread to eat, has been quoted in Sahih al-Bukhari (without a chain of narration). That some great men are seen to reckon one ‘mudd’ (as much as one gets scooping with both hands put together) of grain for one person's food is understood to be an approximate determination of it. That it is enough even if the food is cooked is indeed what is understood from the statement of Anas (r). **والله اعلم**

*[p. 464]* (9) It is as a general principle that the sentence **وَمَن تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا فَهُوَ خَيْرلَهُ** *[TN: The book prints وَمَن “and whoever”; verse 184 has فَمَن “then whoever”.]* (whoever does a good thing of his own accord, it will be excellent for him) stands. Even so, when looking at the context, its import will be: in case one gives something more than one person's food, or keeps the fast itself, even if bearing a little trouble, or else, along with keeping the fast, also gives food to a needy person, that is more meritorious.

(10) That even on an occasion when one can be exempted from the obligation of fasting by giving a ransom, one should indeed try as far as possible to observe fasting, Allah specially alerts: **وَأَنْتَصُومُواخَيْرٌلَكُمْ** (your observing fasting is more excellent for you.) When looking at the gains and rewards to be obtained through fasting in comparison, that it is mightier than the good that is to be obtained through the ransom, and that one must not lose that goodness, remembering the hardship and trouble of the moment – that is the import. The word **إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ** (if you know) also urges the true believers to think about this fact. How mighty the importance of fasting, and the place that Allah assigns to it, are can be understood more or less from a saying of the Prophet ﷺ. He says: ‘For all the deeds of man, a reward from tenfold up to seven-hundredfold will be given. Allah says: Except fasting – it is for Me – it is I who will give the reward for it. He gives up his desire and his food because of Me.’ (Bu; Mu.)

(11) The sentence **شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي** …. *[DOUBT: the dots are printed at the left end of the Arabic, touching it; whether they belong before or after it cannot be told.]* (the month of Ramadan is the month in which the Quran was sent down), along with making clear the intent of having said, in the previous verse, ‘days counted in number’ (**أَيَّامًامَعْدُودَاتٍ**), also points out the reason for choosing that month for fasting. As Allah has made clear, the holy Quran is something that came down for giving guidance to the human race, and for giving discrimination between truth and untruth, surely. In the respect that that divine scripture came down in that month, and in the respect that it serves to keep alive the remembrance of the sending down of the Quran, Allah has chosen that month. That the sending down of the other divine books too was in this same month indeed has also come in some hadiths. In any case, when it is so that fasting was prescribed in the month in which the Quran came down, it can be understood that in that month more consideration needs to be given to the reading of the Quran, and to its study. Hadiths also give support to this. That in every Ramadan Jibril (a) used to come to the Prophet ﷺ and go over the Quran lesson, and that in the last Ramadan that happened twice, having come in hadith is also well known, surely.

That the Quran came down on ‘Lailat al-Qadr’ in Surah Qadr, and that it was on a blessed night in Surah Dukhan, Allah has stated. *[p. 465]* As for the sending down of the Quran being completed, it was over twenty-three years, too. Then, it may be asked what the meaning is of having said that the Quran came down in that month. We are explaining this in those two surahs. The summary is this: Lailat al-Qadr and the blessed night are one indeed. That night is in Ramadan, too. The import of saying that it came down on that night and in that month. That it came down on that night from the ‘Lauh al-Mahfuz’ (**اللوحُ المَحْفُوظ**), which is Allah's record of knowledge, to the nearest heaven, and afterwards came down from there in instalments, is what most Quran commentators state. On the contrary, that the Quran first began to come down in Ramadan is its import – is indeed the opinion of another section. Moreover, there is no objection to both these opinions being correct. **والله اعلم**

(12) After pointing out the principle of having put fasting in Ramadan, Allah says: **فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ** (therefore, whoever was present in that month, he, let him observe fasting in it). That in the early period, when the law of fasting came into being, even those who had the ability to keep the fast could, if they wished, make do with the ransom, and that afterwards it was this that came to be taken away by means of this verse, is the opinion of most scholars – this was said earlier, surely. According to this opinion, this verse takes away that first freedom, and enjoins that in Ramadan everyone must indeed keep the fast. When it comes to be that all who are present in that month must keep the fast, it is so that a doubt might not arise whether this command would be applicable to the sick, and the travellers too, who were given exemption in the previous ayah, that about them, again once more, in this verse ...... **وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَى سَفَرٍ** *[DOUBT: the dots are printed at the left end of the Arabic, touching it; whether they belong before or after it cannot be told.]* (whoever was sick, or on a journey, so many in number from other days) has been said repeatedly – they also say. According to the second opinion, what is intended by this repetition will be only to repeat once more and confirm that matter stated in the previous ayah. What seems more fitting is indeed the majority opinion.

The intent of having said ‘to be present in that month’ is indeed: when that month comes, to be in one's own land, being healthy. That the sick and the travellers have relaxation in fasting, and that fasting is not obligatory for children and for those who have no power of discernment, and that fasting is forbidden for those who have the blood of childbirth and menstrual blood, has also been known by other proofs, surely. For every individual who has not been specially given exemption – in case he is alive during the period of the month of Ramadan – keeping the fast is obligatory. Giving a ransom in place of fasting is not enough – in short.

(13) That, in case one becomes sick or is on a journey, as many days' fasting as was exempted on account of it, so many fasts must *[p. 466]* be observed on other days – following the command of this, having said ..... **يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ بِكُمُ ٱلۡيُسۡرَ** *[DOUBT: the dots are printed at the left end of the Arabic, touching it; whether they belong before or after it cannot be told.]* (Allah intends ease with you – that convenience be brought for you: and He does not intend straitness with you) is specially noteworthy. What this sentence denotes is a general principle that Allah Himself has adopted, and a mighty favour of His. Commanding that, when water is not obtained for wudu' and for bathing, one must do ‘tayammum, with earth, Allah says in Su: Ma'idah; **مَا يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ لِيَجۡعَلَ عَلَيۡكُم مِّنۡ حَرَجٖ** (Allah does not intend to make any hardship upon you. (Ma'idah : 7). *[TN: In the Quran text used here these words are in 5:6.]* After commanding that one must wage struggle in Allah's path in the proper manner, He says in Surah Hajj: **وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيۡكُمۡ فِي ٱلدِّينِ مِنۡ حَرَجٖ** (He has not placed any hardship upon you in the matter of religion (Hajj: 78) **بعثت بالحنيفية السمحة** (I have been sent with a way that is accommodating – broad – upright) – this and the like the noble Prophet ﷺ too has declared. The gist of these is that in the religion there are no commands at all that it is not possible for men to practise; that the religious rulings are there only with the capacity and the circumstances of men being taken into consideration, and with the necessary accommodations being permitted, and so on. What is indicated here is that Allah does not intend to put you in difficulty by compelling that fasting must indeed be observed even when there is sickness, and when one is on a journey. That is why it has been laid down that it is enough even if one completes the number on other days.

Some people who are untouched by religious awareness in their hearts, and who do not know about religious principles as they should, are wont to take this principle – the principle that Allah does not intend straitness with you – as a weapon in order to get past the religious commands in accordance with their own desire. On hearing their argument it will seem that there is no religious ruling at all in Islam that may seem to be a little straitness or difficulty, and that the meaning of this is that each one has freedom to carry out religious observances according to his own wish and convenience! **معاذ الله**

(14) After having said that the fast that was missed must be fasted on other days to pay it off, and that it was so that you may get through without straitness that it was determined thus, look at how Allah concludes this subject: **وَلِتُكۡمِلُواْ ٱلۡعِدَّةَ......وَلَعَلَّكُمۡ تَشۡكُرُونَ** (so that you may complete the number, and so that you may do glorification of Allah on account of His having given you guidance, and so that you may show thanks.) From this, some important matters need to be understood. For example:

(One) It is that, when Allah commands a thing, in case one is unable to bring it about in its perfect form, one must try to be, as far as one can, as He commanded. That if I disallow you concerning some thing, you must avoid it (exactly so), and that if I command you concerning some *[p. 467]* thing, you must bring about from it as much as you can – that the Prophet ﷺ declared this, having come in a strong hadith, is worth remembering here. Be heedful of Allah, you, as much as you are able – thus (64: 16) Allah too has said. The gist of all this is that, on the ground of not being able to bring about an obligatory deed, or a command, entirely in its true form, it is not allowed to leave it off altogether, and that one must take care to complete, as far as one can, its sides, which are the outward and the inward.

(Two) It is that, when one has finished doing a good deed, remembering Allah, and showing thanks to Allah for having favoured one to do that good thing, is necessary. It is to **تَكْبِير** (takbir) that the meaning ‘glorification’ was given. It is the extolment ‘Allahu Akbar’ (Allah is the very greatest Great One) that is ordinarily called ‘takbir’. That feeling gladness about it when one is able to finish doing a goodness, and feeling aversion about it if one happens to do a bad thing, is a sign of the true believer, has also come in hadith.

So, the principle contained in this is that one should display, through the remembrance of Allah, the thanks too for having been able to observe fasting for a month's period, giving up comforts and conveniences for Allah's sake. Not only in fasting, but in the other acts of ritual observance too, this principle needs to be heeded. Allah says below (in the 200th verse) that, when the deed of Hajj has been performed, you must remember Allah as you remember your fathers, or more than that. That, when the Jumu'ah prayer has been performed, one must spread out in the land and seek Allah's favour, and keep Allah in mind much (Surah Jumu'ah : 10), and that after the prayer one must do singing of praise to Allah (Surah Qaf: 40), Allah has stated. That the Prophet ﷺ advised, and practised, many ‘dhikrs’ after the deeds of prayer is also well known, surely.

(Three) A hint too can be seen in this, that the day of the Lesser Festival, which comes about along with the ending of the Ramadan fast, is a day of takbir and of thanks, and of gladness. **والله اعلم**

Although fasting is not obligatory on journeys, whether keeping the fast or not keeping it is better – on this subject a difference of opinion can be seen among the scholars. If examined in the light of the hadiths concerning this, no justification is seen for confirming exactly any one of those opinions. What is generally understood is that, according to the occasion, and the circumstances, and the situation of the individuals, sometimes practising the fast, and sometimes leaving off the fast, will be good. (Allah knows) By way of example, some hadiths may be quoted:-

1. Jabir (r) says: ‘On the journey of the conquest of Makkah, until *[p. 468]* arriving at the place called Kura' al-Ghamim (\*), the Prophet ﷺ kept the Ramadan fast. When he arrived there, the noble Prophet commanded that a cup of water be brought. Raising it up so that the people would look and see, he drank it, too. Afterwards, when it was said that some people were continuing the fast, the noble Prophet said: They are ones who show disobedience! They are ones who show disobedience!’ (Mu; Ti.) That afterwards they too broke the fast, there is a riwayah.

> **Note:** (\*) Kura' al-Ghamim ( **كُرَاعُ الغَمِيم** ) is three nazhikas *[DOUBT: നാഴിക, a traditional Kerala measure (of time, and used also of distance); left untranslated.]* away from 'Usfani (**عُسْفَانِ**). 'Usfan too is situated on the road to Madinah, two ‘marhala:’ (two days' travelling distance) away from Makkah. ( **القاموس** )

2. Anas (r) says: We were on a journey together with the Prophet ﷺ. Among us there were those who were fasting, and those who were not fasting. Then, on a hot day, we alighted at a halting place. People who had a cloak in their possession were the people among us who got the most shade. Some would ward off the sun with their hands. Then, those who were fasting became exhausted. Those who were not fasting rose up and pitched tents, and gave water to the riding animals. Then, the Prophet ﷺ said: ‘Those who did not keep the fast have carried off – today – the reward!’ (Bu; Mu; Na.)

3. In another hadith that Anas (r) quoted, he has also stated that those who were fasting did not blame those who were not, and those who were not, those who were. (Ma; Bu; Mu; Da.)

4. Jabir (r) says: On a journey, the Prophet ﷺ was there (together with us). He saw that people had gathered by a man, and had made shade over him. He asked what was the matter with him. They answered that he was one who was fasting. Then the Rasul ﷺ said: ‘Observing fasting on a journey is not among the virtuous deeds.’ (Ma; Bu; Mu; Da; Na:)

5. A'ishah (r) says: ‘Hamzah ibn 'Amr al-Aslami (r) asked the Prophet ﷺ about fasting on a journey. He was a person who used to observe fasting very much. Then, the Prophet ﷺ said: ‘If you wish, observe the fast; if you wish, leave off the fast.’ (Ma; Bu; Mu; Da; Ti;Na.)

6. Abu al-Darda' (r) says: ‘On a day of severe heat, such that, because of the severity of the heat, one would go about with one's hand on one's head, we set out on a journey in Ramadan together with the Prophet ﷺ. Among us there were none who were fasting except the noble Rasul ﷺ and Ibn Rawahah (r).’ (Bu; Mu; Da.)

7. Hamzah ibn 'Amr al-Aslami (r) said: ‘O Rasul of Allah, I find myself to be one who has strength for fasting on a journey. Therefore, is there any wrong for me in it?’ Then the noble Prophet said: ‘That is a relaxation from Allah, the Majestic and Great. If anyone accepts it, it is good. If a person likes to observe fasting, there is no wrong upon him either.’ (Mu) It will be about this that, in the 5th hadith, A'ishah *[p. 469]* (r) made the statement. **الله اعلم**

Many other hadiths like this are there to quote, too. For understanding the reality, these indeed are enough, surely. Taking into account only some one of these hadiths or events, pronouncing a ruling in general that for the traveller fasting is good, or that not observing fasting is good, will not be correct. It is taking into account the occasion of each one too that the reality must be understood.

In the attitude that making use of the relaxations and accommodations Allah has given is not good, the disposition that undergoes difficulties by itself, and, on the contrary, the disposition that tries to slip out of the law in the name of the relaxation Allah has given, making use of loopholes that are seen, even if only nominal – that both indeed are not good, and that the first is a kind of ingratitude, and the second a kind of defiance, can be understood from all this. Even indeed if a person has a fitting reason to leave off fasting, when that reason is one that others cannot understand, he must take care not to make public his doing things that do not suit fasting, such as eating food. Especially among non-Muslims this needs to be heeded. Otherwise, that it will become the cause of misunderstandings in vain is not the only ill in this. In a way, it is a disrespect towards the month of Ramadan, and something that gives occasion for those of a different religion to feel mockery towards Islam. May He favour us all by including us in the company of the God-fearing who properly observe Allah's injunctions and prohibitions. **آمين**
