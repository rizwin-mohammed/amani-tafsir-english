## 1. Suratul Fatihah

**سورة الفاتحة** · Revealed in Madinah – Verses 7 *\[TN: the heading reads "Madinah", but the text below states the correct view is that it was revealed in early Makkah. The heading is highlighted in the PDF, perhaps for this reason.\]* *\[p. 145\]*

As the surah (chapter) that is read first in the Holy Quran and written first in the 'Mus'hafs', it is called 'Suratul Fatihah' (**سورة الفاتحة**), that is, the Opening Chapter, and 'Fatihatul Kitab' (**فاتحة الكتاب**), that is, the Opening of the Scripture. In the sense of the root, that is, the centre, of the Quran, it is also named 'Ummul Quran' and 'Ummul Kitab' (**ام القرآن، ام الكتاب**). The main subjects of the Quran are fundamental doctrines such as the most exalted attributive names of Allah, the doctrine of the Oneness of God (Tawhid), life after death and the process of recompense; practical and moral rules and directions; historical events; and the like. A summary of all of these is contained in this surah. This is what these two names indicate. The word 'Umm' takes meanings such as 'source of origin, centre, principal part, rallying point, mother, vital core', according to the context.

Apart from the names mentioned above, many other names are found for this surah in the Hadiths and in the sayings of great men among the Companions (Sahabah) and the Successors (Tabi'un). All of them point to its importance and excellence in one way or another. Among them are 'Asasul Quran' (**اساس القرآن** – the foundation of the Quran), since it contains the fundamental aspects of Quranic knowledge; 'Kafiyah' (**الكافية** – the sufficient), in the sense that for those who study attentively and pursue the details further, this surah itself suffices to grasp the essential matters; and 'Kanz' (**الكنز** – the treasure), meaning a treasury of the values of knowledge. In addition, because its opening is a hymn of praise setting out the glorious attributes of Allah, it is called **سورة الحمد** (the chapter of praise); *\[p. 146\]* because its last part is in the form of a prayer, **سورة الدعاء** (the chapter of supplication); and because it cannot be omitted from the prayer (salah), **سورة الصلوة** (the chapter of the prayer).

The correct view is that this surah was revealed in the early phase of the Makkan life of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Allah says: **وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَاكَ سَبْعًا مِّنَ الْمَثَانِي وَالْقُرْآنَ الْعَظِيمَ** – الحجر ("Assuredly, We have given you seven of the oft-repeated verses, and the Mighty Quran" – Al-Hijr 87). That the 'seven oft-repeated verses' (**السبع المثانى**) refers to Suratul Fatihah is made clear by a saying of the Prophet recorded by Imam Ahmad, Bukhari (r) and others from Abu Sa'id ibnul Mu'alla (r). There is no difference of opinion that Suratul Fatihah consists of seven ayahs (verses). In the five obligatory prayers, every Muslim has to recite it at least seventeen times daily. In sunnah prayers and elsewhere it is repeated tens and hundreds of times more. From this one can understand the secret behind this surah being called 'the seven oft-repeated verses', and behind this one surah being singled out by name in that verse. There is no doubt that Suratul Hijr is one of the Makkan surahs. So this surah must have been revealed even before it. The word **المثانى** (the oft-repeated) has also been given other interpretations. We will explain that in its proper place, **إن شاء الله**.

In the Hadith from Abu Sa'id ibnul Mu'alla (r) mentioned above, the Prophet ﷺ asked him, 'Shall I teach you the greatest surah in the Quran?', and then told him, 'It is Suratul Fatihah' and 'It is the seven oft-repeated verses'. In another Hadith narrated by Ahmad, Tirmidhi (r) and others through Ubayy ibn Ka'b (r), it is reported that the Prophet ﷺ declared: 'Neither in the Torah, nor in the Injil, nor in the Zabur, nor in the Furqan (the Quran) has a surah like this been revealed.' A saying of the Prophet narrated by Abu Hurairah (r) runs thus: 'Whoever performs any prayer in which he has not recited Ummul Quran, it is incomplete, it is incomplete, it is incomplete' (Mu., Na., Ti.). Another saying of the Prophet, 'There is no prayer for one who does not recite Fatihatul Kitab', has been recorded by Bukhari and Muslim (r) from Ubadah ibn as-Samit (**عبادة بن اصامت – رضـ**). It is on the basis of such Hadiths that the great majority of scholars, including Malik, Shafi'i and Ahmad (r), have firmly held that reciting the Fatihah is obligatory in every 'rak'ah' of the prayer. *\[p. 147\]* Although much more could be quoted about the excellence and greatness of this surah, we do not lengthen this by quoting further.

## Saying 'A'udhu' (reciting it), that is, seeking refuge – الاستعاذة

The supplication **أعوذ بالله من الشيطان الرجيم** (A'udhu-billahi-minash-shaitanir-rajim) is what is commonly called saying 'A'udhu'. Its meaning is: 'I seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the driven-away, that is, the accursed.' Satan is man's lifelong enemy, and the one most envious of his well-being. The aim of this supplication is to seek protection from Allah against every kind of harm that can come from Satan. Man has many kinds of enemies within mankind itself, and we need to pray to Allah for protection from those enemies too. But the harms that come from human enemies, and the remedies for them, we can largely anticipate and understand. Satan, on the other hand, is an invisible being whom we cannot see.(\*) His doings and harms are so hidden that we can neither detect them with our outer senses nor pin them down with our intellect. Satan is the one who vowed before Allah that he would use every trick in his power to lead mankind astray. Allah has made us aware of this fact again and again through the Quran. (See places such as Al-A'raf 16, 17; Al-Hijr 39; Al-Kahf 50; Fatir 6; Sad 82.) In short, protection from Satan has to come to us from Allah Himself.

If one examines the Quranic verses that counsel us, side by side, about the ways of protection from the harms of both kinds of enemies, men and devils, the truth stated above becomes clear: the only way to be saved from Satan's harms is to seek protection with Allah. As Imam Ibn Kathir (r) has pointed out, there are three places in the Quran that explain together the ways of protection from both kinds of enemies. They are:

> ١) خُذِ الْعَفْوَ وَأْمُرْ بِالْعُرْفِ وَأَعْرِضْ عَنِ الْجَاهِلِينَ ﴿١٩٩﴾ وَإِمَّا يَنزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ ﴿٢٠٠﴾ – الأعراف
>
> *\[p. 148\]* ٢) ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ ۚ نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَصِفُونَ ﴿٩٦﴾ وَقُل رَّبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ ﴿٩٧﴾ وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَن يَحْضُرُونِ ﴿٩٨﴾ – المؤمنون

**Gist:**

1. Take to forgiveness, enjoin decency (that is, good conduct), and turn away from ignorant fools. And if ever some evil prompting from Satan stirs you, seek refuge with Allah. Surely, He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing. (Al-A'raf 199–200)
2. Repel the evil by means of that which is better. We know best what they describe. And say: 'My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the evil whisperings of the devils, and I seek refuge in You, (my Lord,) lest they come near me.' (Al-Mu'minun 96–98)
3. Repel (the evil) with that which is better. If you do so, the one between whom and you there was enmity will become as though he were a close, intimate friend. None is granted this except those who are patient; none is granted this except one possessed of great fortune. And if ever some evil prompting from Satan stirs you, seek refuge in Allah. Surely, He alone is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. (Ha Mim Sajdah 34–36) *\[TN: Ha Mim Sajdah is Surah Fussilat, chapter 41.\]*

If the enemy is of mankind, he can be won over by gentleness, tact, concession and kindness, and one's relationship with him can be put right. When the enemy is Satan, because he is an invisible being, there is no way of gentleness, tact or anything else with him. It is clear from these verses that the only recourse for being saved from him is to seek refuge in Allah. We must always be on our guard about Satan. Allah says: **إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ فَاتَّخِذُوهُ** *\[p. 149\]* **عَدُوًّا ۚ إِنَّمَا يَدْعُو حِزْبَهُ لِيَكُونُوا مِنْ أَصْحَابِ السَّعِيرِ** – فاطر (Surely, Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy. He calls his party only so that they may become among the inmates of the blazing Fire – Fatir 6). So, generally on every occasion, and especially on occasions when we are liable to give in to Satan's evil promptings, we must seek protection with Allah against Satan.

Allah says: ( **فَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ** – النحل:٩٨ ) 'When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed' (An-Nahl 98). The view held on the basis of this verse by the great majority of scholars, and generally accepted, is that one should say 'A'udhu' when beginning to recite the Quran. Hadith also supports this view. It helps keep Satan's evil whisperings from affecting us in a way that spoils the purpose of reciting the Quran. That it is good to say 'A'udhu' wherever there is occasion for his evil whisperings can be understood from a Hadith narrated by Bukhari (r) and others. The relevant part of the Hadith may be quoted thus: 'Two men exchanged harsh words in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ. Out of anger, one man's face turned red. Then the Prophet ﷺ said: "I know a word. If he said it, the anger seen in him would leave him. It is: **أعوذ بالله من الشيطان الرجيم**."'

Taking the outward wording of this Quranic verse, some have even held that saying 'A'udhu' when reciting the Quran is obligatory. It cannot be called obligatory; but what the great majority of scholars say is that it is an important voluntary act of virtue. When the recitation is in prayer, 'A'udhu' should be said quietly. Only when one reflects on how many, how hidden and how grave are the dangers that can befall us from Satan, and remembers that the only safeguard against them is Allah's protection, can one value the weight of the supplication 'A'udhu' as it deserves.

## Saying 'Bismi', that is, beginning in the name of Allah – البسملة

In every Mus'haf, from the first Mus'haf compiled in writing in the time of Abu Bakr Siddiq (r) onward, all 113 surahs other than Suratut Tawbah begin with 'Bismi'. (The reason Surah Tawbah has no Bismi has been indicated in the Preface. More details will be seen in its proper place, **إن شاء الله**.) In addition, *\[p. 150\]* verse 30 of Suratun Naml also contains a 'Bismi'. No one disputes that that Bismi is part of that verse. Among scholars there are differing views on whether the Bismis at the beginnings of the surahs belong to the Quran or not. In brief, they are these:

1. The Bismi in each surah is the first ayah of that surah.
2. The Bismi is part of the first ayah of the respective surah.
3. Only in Suratul Fatihah is it the first ayah. In the other surahs it has been placed at the beginning to distinguish the surahs from one another.
4. They mark the beginning of all surahs, the Fatihah included; that is, none of them is an ayah of its surah.

To quote the reasons and evidence that the holders of each view cite from the Hadiths and elsewhere would make this very long.

One thing can be said with certainty. When beginning to recite the Quran, and at the beginning of surahs (at the beginning of the Fatihah especially), saying 'Bismi' is required. The first Quranic verse revealed to the Prophet ﷺ was **اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ** (Read in the name of your Lord). The Prophet ﷺ has shown us the practical form of beginning in the Lord's name: the 'Bismi'. When asked about the Prophet's ﷺ recitation of the Quran, Anas (r) declared that he used to recite with prolongation, and then demonstrated it by saying 'Bismillahi', 'Ar-Rahmani' and 'Ar-Rahimi', drawing each out; Bukhari (r) has recorded this. From it we understand that the Prophet recited the Quran together with the Bismi. Moreover, in a Hadith narrated from Umm Salamah (r) by Ahmad, Abu Dawud, Hakim (r) and others, it is reported that she said his recitation was broken up (reciting without running the ayahs together), and that she demonstrated it by reciting the Bismi and two ayahs of the Fatihah.

Not only when reciting the Quran, but whenever beginning any good and desirable task, one should begin in the name of Allah, that is, with the Bismi. This is something generally known from the practice of the Prophet ﷺ. There are also Hadiths instructing the saying of the Bismi on particular occasions. Allah has declared that Prophet Nuh (a), when he boarded the ship by Allah's command to be saved from the Flood, said 'Bismillahi' (Hud 41), and that Prophet Sulaiman (a) wrote the 'Bismi' in full at the head of the letter he sent to the Queen of Yemen (Naml 30). From all this it can be understood that beginning important matters with the Bismi is a custom *\[p. 151\]* going back to the earlier prophets.

Anyone who reflects a little will understand that beginning good deeds in the name of Allah holds many wisdoms. It is Allah who gives the urge, the ability and the circumstances to do good. The Bismi is a remembrance of this favour and an expression of gratitude for it. Help to carry out the task as it should be done, protection from things that might hinder it (especially protection from Satan's interference), and the good fortune of that deed being accepted by Allah: all of these must come from Allah alone. In that respect, that auspicious beginning, made while remembering and pronouncing His name and His gracious favour, becomes a cause for the outcome to turn out well. Suppose a person does a good deed. If he does it without any thought of Allah at that moment, he has no claim to Allah's pleasure or reward on account of that deed.

When in prayer, is it better to say the Bismi aloud or quietly? On this there is a difference of opinion among scholars. The causes of this division are that no conclusive evidence for either is found in the Hadiths, and the differing views on whether the Bismi is an ayah of the Quran or not. However, nobody says that saying the Bismi aloud or quietly in prayer spoils the prayer. Nor does anyone hold that one of the two is obligatory. The difference is only over which of the two is better. Imam Ibnul Qayyim (r), in his famous biography of the Prophet, Zadul Ma'ad (**زاد المعاد**), while describing the form of the Prophet's ﷺ prayer, states thus: 'Then he would say the A'udhu, then recite Surah Fatihah. Sometimes he would say the Bismi aloud. More often than aloud, he used to say it quietly. It is surely impossible that he said it aloud regularly, five times every day and night, on journeys and at home, and yet it remained unknown to the Rightly Guided Caliphs, to the majority of the Companions and to the people of that blessed era...' Just as we said about 'A'udhu', we do not lengthen this by quoting here the differences of opinion about the Bismi and the justifications each view bases on Hadiths or other grounds. (The books of Fiqh are their place.) The Hadiths that could be quoted are, as Ibnul Qayyim (r) said, either unclear on the matter or weak.

*\[p. 152\]* Even so, we can understand this much from them:

1. Whether or not each Bismi at the beginning of a surah is an ayah of the surah or part of an ayah, they are in general included in the Quran, and they also mark the beginnings of the surahs. The proof of this is that the 'Bismi' has been written at the beginning of every surah except Tawbah in all Mus'hafs, from the first copy of the Mus'haf prepared by Uthman (r) until now.
2. When reciting the Quran, one should first say 'A'udhu' and then the 'Bismi'.
3. Each surah should be recited together with the 'Bismi'.
4. In prayer, 'A'udhu' should be recited quietly. The 'Bismi' may be said quietly or aloud, but quietly is better. **الله أعلم** (Allah knows best).
